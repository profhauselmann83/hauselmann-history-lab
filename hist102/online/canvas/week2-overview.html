Week 2 · October 26 – November 1

The Gilded Age and Industrialization

OpenStax Ch. 17–18, 20  ·  The Bill of Obligations: "Promote the Common Good"

Welcome to Week 2. This week we enter the Gilded Age, the decades from the end of Reconstruction to the turn of the twentieth century, when the United States became the world's leading industrial power. Steel mills, railroads, oil refineries, and telephone lines remade the American landscape almost overnight. Andrew Carnegie, John D. Rockefeller, and J. P. Morgan built fortunes on a scale the country had never seen. Mark Twain called it a "gilded" age for a reason: a glittering surface hid deep social tensions underneath.

You'll meet the people who paid for that growth: factory workers who organized, struck, and were sometimes met with gunfire at Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman; farmers squeezed by railroads, banks, and the gold standard who rose up in the Populist revolt; and homesteaders, miners, and cowboys pushing west. Above all, you'll meet the Native nations whose land and ways of life the federal government set out to take, from Little Bighorn to the Dawes Act to Wounded Knee. The Gilded Age was a story of remarkable opportunity and stark inequality, often in the same place at the same time.

Central Question

Who paid the price for America's industrial and territorial expansion? As you read, keep asking who benefited and at whose expense: factory workers, farmers, immigrants, and Native Americans all lived through this transformation very differently from the men whose names were on the railroads and steel mills.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • Explain how industrialization and technological innovation transformed the U.S. economy between 1870 and 1900
  • Analyze the rise of big business and the ideas used to justify it, including Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth"
  • Evaluate the strategies and struggles of the early labor movement, from the Knights of Labor to the AFL
  • Assess the causes of the farmers' revolt and the influence of the Populist movement on American politics
  • Examine westward migration: who went west, why, and with what consequences for settlers and Native peoples
  • Analyze federal Indian policy, including the reservation system, the Dawes Act, and the Ghost Dance movement
  • Connect Haass's obligation to "Promote the Common Good" to the era's debates over wealth, labor, and land

Bill of Obligations

"Promote the Common Good"

Haass argues that citizens owe one another more than the pursuit of their own interests. A healthy democracy depends on people weighing what is good for the whole community, not only for themselves. This week, ask yourself: whose good counted as the "common" good in the Gilded Age?

Carnegie claimed great fortunes served the common good through philanthropy. Labor organizers and Populists argued the opposite, that concentrated wealth threatened the republic. Their debate is still with us.
