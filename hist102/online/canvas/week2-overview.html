The Gilded Age and Industrialization

OpenStax Ch. 17–18, 20  ·  The Bill of Obligations: "Promote the Common Good"

Welcome to Week 2. This week we enter the Gilded Age, the decades between the end of Reconstruction and the turn of the twentieth century, when the United States became the world's leading industrial power. Steel mills, railroads, oil refineries, and telephone lines remade the American landscape almost overnight, and men like Andrew Carnegie, John D. Rockefeller, and J. P. Morgan built fortunes on a scale the country had never seen. Mark Twain called it a "gilded" age for a reason — a glittering surface concealing deep social tensions beneath.

You'll meet the people who paid for that growth this week: factory workers who organized, struck, and were sometimes met with gunfire at Haymarket, Homestead, and Pullman; farmers squeezed by railroads, banks, and the gold standard who rose up in the Populist revolt; and homesteaders, miners, and cowboys pushing west. Above all, you'll confront the experience of Native nations whose land and ways of life the federal government set out to take — from Little Bighorn to the Dawes Act to Wounded Knee. The Gilded Age was remarkable opportunity and stark inequality, often in the same place at the same time.

Central Question

Who paid the price for America's industrial and territorial expansion? As the nation grew richer and larger, keep asking who benefited — and at whose expense: factory workers, farmers, immigrants, and Native Americans.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this week, you will be able to:

  • Explain how industrialization and technological innovation transformed the U.S. economy between 1870 and 1900
  • Analyze the rise of big business and the ideas used to justify it, including Carnegie's "Gospel of Wealth"
  • Evaluate the struggles of the early labor movement and the causes of the Populist revolt
  • Assess the motives and consequences of westward migration, including federal Indian policy, the Dawes Act, and the Ghost Dance
  • Connect Haass's obligation to "Promote the Common Good" to Gilded-Age debates over wealth, labor, and land

Bill of Obligations

"Promote the Common Good"

Haass argues that citizens owe one another more than the pursuit of their own interests — democracy depends on people weighing what is good for the whole community. This week, ask yourself: Whose good counted as the "common good" in the Gilded Age?

Carnegie claimed great fortunes served everyone through philanthropy; labor organizers and Populists argued that concentrated wealth threatened the republic itself.
